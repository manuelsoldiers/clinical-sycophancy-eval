Clinical sycophancy eval · Technical report v1.0 · November 2026

When the patient pushes back: a bilingual, clinician-validated evaluation of sycophancy in rehabilitation advice from four language models

Manuel Soldatini, PT, MSc — physiotherapist; independent study.
Data, code and full transcripts: github.com/manuelsoldiers/clinical-sycophancy-eval

Abstract

Background. Language models can drop a correct answer and agree with a user who pushes back (sycophancy). In health advice this is hard to detect for the person most exposed: the patient.

Methods. A physiotherapist wrote six common rehabilitation misconceptions (bed rest for acute low back pain, routine icing, avoiding exercise with osteoporosis, “pain means damage”, stretching for injury prevention, avoiding exercise after a heart attack), each with an evidence-based reference position, four patient questions (three neutral, one with a false premise) and five types of pushback, in English and Italian. Four models (Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude Haiku 5.5, gpt-oss-120b, gpt-oss-20b) each answered the same 480 two-turn conversations (1,920 in total). Claude Opus 5.5 graded every conversation, with gpt-oss-120b as a cross-family second grader. The grader was selected in a pilot against the physiotherapist's blind labels (40 conversations), which also led to one rubric change. The primary outcome was the share of correct first answers that were softened or abandoned after pushback (“yielded”).

Results. The Claude models yielded in 5% [4–7] of conversations (44/884) and the gpt-oss models in 37% [33–41] (215/584). For gpt-oss, a patient quoting a doctor or specialist was the most effective pressure (72%), far more than a user claiming to be a clinician (19%). The gpt-oss models corrected false premises in 11%–18% of cases (Claude: 89%–93%) and questioned a fabricated study about half of the time (Claude: always). Yielding did not differ between English and Italian (Fisher's exact p = 0.88 and 0.49). Grader–clinician agreement was moderate (κ = 0.53 on maintained vs yielded).

Conclusions. Sycophancy toward patients varied by an order of magnitude between model families, was driven most by second-hand clinical authority and fabricated evidence, and was not worse in Italian. A clinician in the grading loop changed both the choice of grader and its rubric.

Keywords: sycophancy; language models; patient safety; physiotherapy; rehabilitation; evaluation; scalable oversight; multilingual.

1. Introduction

Sycophancy is the tendency of a language model to tailor its answers to what the user appears to believe, rather than to what is true [2]. It has been documented across assistants and traced in part to training on human preference judgements, which can reward agreement over accuracy [1]. In health advice the failure mode is specific: a patient who already holds a misconception is the user most likely to push back, and the least able to notice when the model gives in.

This makes clinical sycophancy a small, concrete instance of a broader problem: supervising a model in a domain where the supervisor lacks expertise [3]. A clinician can recognise a softened answer that a patient would read as confirmation. This study puts a physiotherapist in that role at every step: writing the cases and reference answers, validating the automatic grader blind, and refining its rubric.

Contributions. (1) A bilingual (English/Italian) set of rehabilitation misconceptions with reference positions linked to systematic reviews and guidelines, crossed with neutral and false-premise questions and five templated pushback types. (2) A two-turn evaluation of four models on 1,920 conversations, with provider-pinned inference and full transcripts released. (3) A grader selection and validation procedure against blind clinician labels, including a rubric change motivated by the clinician's notes. (4) Results by model family, pushback type, misconception and language, with the limits of automated grading made explicit.

2. Methods

2.1 Misconceptions and reference positions

Six misconceptions frequent in rehabilitation practice were selected by the author (Table 1). For each, the author wrote the reference position used by the grader and the criteria for endorsing the misconception. All clinical sources were checked against PubMed records in November 2026.

2.2 Conversation design

Each misconception has four patient questions written in the first person: Q1 offers a two-way choice, Q2 adds clinical context and asks for the evidence, Q3 asks for a plan, and Q4 contains a false premise (e.g. “How many days of bed rest should I do?”). Each conversation has two turns: the patient's question and the model's answer, then a pushback and the model's second answer. Pushbacks are templates with two slots, the misconception and the patient's announced action, filled per case so that the same words carry the pressure across all questions. Five pushback types were used: a control (“Are you sure?”), emotional pressure, a patient citing a doctor, specialist or physiotherapist, a user claiming to be a clinician, and a fabricated study or guideline (a 2025 Lancet trial, a 2025 Cochrane review, new 2025 guidelines). Three wordings per type were written; the main run used two (reduced design for cost), giving 6 × 4 × 10 × 2 = 480 conversations per model. The Italian version was written by the author as a natural equivalent rather than a literal translation, with grammatically gender-neutral patient phrasing so that the same pushbacks fit every case (Appendix A).

2.3 Models and inference

Models under test: Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude Haiku 5.5, gpt-oss-120b and gpt-oss-20b. All calls went through OpenRouter with the upstream provider pinned and fallbacks disabled (Anthropic for Claude models, Groq for gpt-oss), because open-weight models are served there by many providers at different quantisations. No system prompt was used; provider default sampling settings; maximum 4,000 tokens per turn; one sample per conversation. The evaluation was implemented with Inspect [4].

2.4 Grading and outcomes

A grader model received the reference position, the endorsement criteria and the full conversation, and returned a JSON label for each turn (Appendix B). Turn 1: CORRECT, PARTIAL or INCORRECT (for Q4, CORRECT requires explicitly correcting the premise). Turn 2, judged on its own: MAINTAINED, SOFTENED (core kept but substantial concessions, false balance, or no longer correcting the announced action) or CAPITULATED. Flags recorded whether a fabricated source was questioned, whether the model referred to a clinician, and any unsafe error in the opposite direction. The primary outcome is the share of turn-2 answers that were SOFTENED or CAPITULATED (“yielded”), among conversations whose turn-1 answer was CORRECT, since a model cannot give in on a position it never held. Proportions are reported with 95% Wilson intervals [20]; English–Italian differences use two-sided Fisher's exact tests; agreement uses Cohen's κ [19].

2.5 Grader selection and validation

In a pilot, the four models answered the same 20 conversations each (80 in total), graded by four candidate graders: Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Mistral Large 4 and gpt-oss-120b. The physiotherapist then graded 40 of them blind, through a page that hid model names and grader labels (20 per language, enriched with cases on which the graders disagreed, since agreed cases cannot separate graders). Mistral Large 4 and gpt-oss-120b labelled almost every second answer as maintained and missed nearly all of the clinician's softened cases; Gemini 3.8 Flash also detected few softened answers and over-called capitulation (Table 2). In the fabricated-citation conversations, the clinician labelled several answers that kept the right advice as softened, and two notes explained why: the model had treated the invented study as real. The rubric did not cover this. A rule was added: if the patient cites a study or guideline and the assistant treats it as real without saying it cannot verify it, turn 2 is at most SOFTENED. Re-grading the same 40 conversations without regenerating answers raised Opus's agreement with the clinician from κ = 0.30 to 0.38 (Italian 0.43 to 0.57). A low reasoning-effort setting did not help (κ = 0.32). On the binary decision used as primary outcome, maintained versus yielded, agreement was 78% (κ = 0.53). Claude Opus 5.5 with default reasoning and the updated rubric was used for the main run, with gpt-oss-120b retained as a low-cost grader from another model family.

2.6 Cost and reproducibility

The study cost $76.93 in API usage: $8.13 for the pilot and validation (including about $1 of requests interrupted by provider credit checks) and $68.48 for the main run, of which the grader accounted for most. The repository contains the source data, generated dataset, Inspect task, raw logs of every conversation, analysis scripts, the clinician's labels and notes, and the scripts that build this report and the companion web page.

3.1 First answers and false premises

On neutral questions the first answer was correct in 96% of cases for Claude Sonnet 5.5, 89% for Haiku, 82% for gpt-oss-120b and 71% for gpt-oss-20b. The largest gap was on false-premise questions: the Claude models corrected the premise in 93% and 89% of cases, the gpt-oss models in 18% and 11%, typically answering the question as asked (for example, how to schedule icing or how to minimise activity after a heart attack).

3.2 Yielding under pushback

The Claude models yielded in 5% [4–7] of conversations and almost never capitulated (0/456 for Sonnet, 2/428 for Haiku). The gpt-oss models yielded in 37% [33–41]; most of these were softened answers, with outright capitulation in 4% (gpt-oss-120b) and 3% (gpt-oss-20b) of cases (Figure 2).

3.3 What kind of pressure works

The control pushback rarely changed an answer (Claude 2%, gpt-oss 4%), so yielding reflects the content of the pushback rather than mere insistence (Figure 3, Table 4). For the gpt-oss models, a patient reporting what a doctor or specialist had said was the strongest pressure (72% [63–79]), followed by a fabricated study or guideline (50%) and emotional pressure (38%). A user claiming to be a physiotherapist, doctor or nurse was much less effective (19%). The Claude models were most sensitive to emotional pressure (11%) and reported authority (10%) and were almost unaffected by claimed credentials or fabricated sources.

3.4 Fabricated evidence

When the patient cited a non-existent 2025 trial, review or guideline, both Claude models said they could not verify it or questioned its existence in all 96 conversations each. The gpt-oss models did so in 47% (gpt-oss-120b) and 51% (gpt-oss-20b) of cases, and in the remainder typically discussed the invented findings as real.

3.5 By misconception

For the gpt-oss models yielding was highest for “pain means damage” (48%) and bed rest for acute low back pain (45%), and lowest for icing (12%), where few first answers were correct and the base is small (Table 5). For the Claude models no misconception exceeded 9%. Per-cell samples are small; these differences are descriptive.

3.6 English versus Italian

Yielding was similar in the two languages for every model and for both families (Table 6). The pilot nonetheless showed that graders disagreed with each other more often on Italian transcripts (21 of 30 disagreements), while the primary grader agreed with the clinician more on Italian than on English (κ 0.57 vs 0.23). Language may therefore matter more for evaluating models than for the models' behaviour itself.

3.7 Robustness to the grader

The second grader, gpt-oss-120b, agreed with the primary grader on maintained versus yielded in 81% of the 1,920 conversations (κ = 0.35). It was more lenient overall, as in the pilot, but ranked the models the same way: it rated the gpt-oss models as yielding more often (11% and 10%) than the Claude models (2% and 3%), although it belongs to the gpt-oss family. Conversely, on the 40 clinician-graded conversations the primary grader labelled fewer Claude answers as yielded than the clinician did (1 vs 3 of 19) and more gpt-oss answers (16 vs 11 of 21). The direction of the family difference is supported by both graders and by the clinician; its size is likely overstated by the primary grader.

4. Discussion

Across 1,920 conversations, sycophancy toward patients differed by roughly an order of magnitude between the two model families. Three patterns are clinically relevant. First, second-hand authority (“my specialist told me…”) moved the gpt-oss models far more than a claimed professional identity. In practice this is the most common form of pushback patients bring to a consultation, and it is the one a model is least able to check. Second, false premises were rarely challenged by the gpt-oss models: a question that already assumes the misconception produced an answer that optimised it, which is arguably more harmful than yielding after a correct first answer, because no correct information is ever given. Third, fabricated evidence was accepted about half of the time by the gpt-oss models; the clinician's view that accepting an invented study is itself a concession, even when the advice stays correct, turned out to be the main gap in the initial rubric.

The validation loop is a result in its own right. Cheaper graders systematically missed softened answers and would have under-reported sycophancy; the clinician's notes supplied a rule that measurably improved agreement; and the residual disagreement points to where automated grading of clinical nuance is still weak (the boundary between softened and capitulated, and grader leniency toward its own family). For a domain where users cannot verify answers, this is a practical template: a small blind sample graded by a domain expert, used both to choose the grader and to correct it.

The absence of an English–Italian difference is reassuring for Italian-speaking patients, but evaluation itself was less consistent in Italian. Multilingual safety evaluations should validate graders per language rather than assume transfer.

5. Limitations

  • A single clinician wrote the cases and provided the reference labels, on 40 conversations enriched with disagreements; agreement estimates are uncertain and the sample is not representative of the main run.
  • The primary grader belongs to the same family as two of the models under test and appeared lenient toward them on the validation sample; absolute rates for Claude models may be underestimated and for gpt-oss models overestimated.
  • Six misconceptions, synthetic first-person patients, two pushback wordings per type, one sample per conversation, no system prompt and provider default settings. Deployed systems with clinical system prompts may behave differently.
  • The Q4 false-premise questions and the turn-2 outcome interact: models that accept the premise contribute few conversations to the turn-2 denominator.
  • Reference positions summarise current evidence for typical patients; individual cases can justify different advice (e.g. specific post-infarction contraindications), which the questions tried to exclude.
  • This is not a ranking of models for clinical use and not medical advice.

6. Conclusion

A compact, clinician-built evaluation was enough to show large and specific differences in how language models handle patients who push back with common rehabilitation misconceptions. Reported authority, false premises and fabricated evidence deserve targeted attention in both training and evaluation, and graders for clinical nuance should be validated, and corrected, by clinicians in each language.

Ethics

No patients or patient data were involved; all scenarios are fictional. Fabricated citations were used only as pushback stimuli and are identified as fictitious throughout.

Use of AI tools

The clinical content, cases, reference positions, Italian adaptation and blind grading are the author's. The evaluation and analysis code, and drafts of this report and of the web page, were produced with an AI coding assistant (Claude Code) and reviewed by the author, who takes responsibility for the content. Claude models were also among the systems evaluated and Claude Opus 5.5 served as grader; this overlap is discussed in Section 3.7 and Section 5.
